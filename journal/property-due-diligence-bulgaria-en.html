The Due Diligence That Saves the Deal

The notary checks whether the seller is the registered owner and whether the property carries registered encumbrances. That's it. Here's what falls outside that check.

Guides 25 September 2026 6 min read
Накратко

In Bulgaria, the notary checks whether the seller is the registered owner and whether the property carries registered encumbrances - that's it. Unregistered claims, boundary disputes, illegal construction and planning violations fall outside that check entirely.

This isn't a criticism of the notarial system - it performs exactly the function it's designed for: authenticating the transaction and checking officially registered circumstances. The problem is that buyers often assume this check is comprehensive when it isn't.

What the notary actually does - and doesn't

Every property transfer in Bulgaria is executed before a notary in the district where the property is located. Before signing, the notary checks the so-called "stop list" in the Property Register - registered encumbrances, mortgages, seizures and pending litigation against the property.

What falls outside that check: unregistered claims, boundary disputes between neighbouring properties, illegal extensions or additions, and violations of the municipality's spatial development plan. If a broker or seller tells you "the notary checks everything" - treat that as a red flag, not reassurance.

What full legal due diligence actually covers

Under Bulgarian legal practice, a full pre-purchase review typically covers:

  • Ownership and chain of title - a clean ownership history, not just the most recent deed
  • Cadastre and planning status - whether actual construction matches approved documents
  • Encumbrances - mortgages, seizures, easements, rights of way
  • Tax and utility debts - unpaid local taxes, electricity, water, heating tied to the property
  • Seller's marital status - spousal consent is required if the seller is married
  • Access - a lawful road to the property, especially for rural homes and plots

When diligence should start

The most common mistake isn't a skipped check - it's a late one. Legal due diligence should begin before signing a preliminary contract and before paying a deposit, not after. Once a preliminary contract is signed, the buyer has already taken on contractual obligations and has weaker negotiating leverage if a problem surfaces later.

An encumbrance certificate from the Registry Agency is an important but insufficient piece of the picture on its own.

An important change in 2026

As of early 2026, public access to detailed Property Register data is restricted. Checks now require authenticated access via the Registry Agency portal (with a qualified electronic signature) or official encumbrance certificates, rather than informal online lookups. In practice, this makes a structured review through a lawyer or licensed partner more essential than before, not less.

Another practical consequence: registration priority now depends on filing speed. A delay between signing the notarial deed and its electronic registration creates exposure to competing claims - same-day filing is now standard good practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the notary's check enough when buying property in Bulgaria?+
No. The notary checks registered ownership and registered encumbrances, but not unregistered claims, boundary disputes, illegal construction or planning violations.
When should legal due diligence happen?+
Before signing a preliminary contract and before paying a deposit - not after.
What does an encumbrance certificate show?+
Only officially registered circumstances as of the issue date - mortgages, seizures, court disputes.
Did the due diligence process change in 2026?+
Yes. Public online access to the Property Register is now restricted and requires authenticated access via a qualified electronic signature.
What is the lawyer's role versus the notary's?+
The notary authenticates the transaction and checks registered circumstances as of the date of the deed. A lawyer or licensed partner carries out the wider review - ownership history, planning status, unregistered claims - before the file ever reaches the notary. The two roles complement each other rather than duplicate.
